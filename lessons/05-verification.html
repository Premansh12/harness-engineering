Harness Engineering · Module 05

Back-pressure

An agent that cannot tell whether its work is correct will confidently tell you it is. This module covers the two halves of the fix: shaping verification signals so success costs nothing and failure is actionable, and separating the agent that does the work from the agent that judges it.

Walkthroughlab/w05_backpressure.py55 minCompiled by Premansh Panigrahi

Section 1Success must be silent

Start with a measurement, because this one is larger than people expect. A test suite with 220 passing tests, run through a harness two ways:

real output · lab/w05_backpressure.py
verbose (dump all)     context after one green run:   10,011 chars
silent on success      context after one green run:       89 chars

Same information content. The agent learns exactly one thing in both cases — everything passed — at a 112x difference in context cost. And that is one run. An agent that verifies twenty times over a long session pays that difference twenty times.

HumanLayer reports the concrete downstream failure: 4,000 lines of passing test output flooded the window and the agent started hallucinating about files it had just read. The verification step, meant to increase reliability, destroyed it.

Success should be silent. Failure should be loud, specific, and actionable.The design contract

What "actionable" means

Not just loud — useful. Compare:

FAILED: 1 test failed
exit=1
tests/test_auth.py::test_expired_token FAILED
E   AssertionError: expected 401, got 200
E   at auth/middleware.py:88

The second contains the file, the line, the expectation, and the actual. The agent can act on it without a round trip to go investigate. Every round trip you save is latency, tokens, and one fewer opportunity to lose the thread.

Böckeler's framing of this is sharp — a sensor that emits signal optimised for LLM consumption is "a positive kind of prompt injection". You are deliberately writing text designed to steer the model, and delivering it through a channel the model cannot ignore.

Section 2Hooks: the part the model cannot skip

Asking the model to run tests works most of the time. Most of the time is not a control. A hook fires deterministically, in harness code, regardless of what the model felt like doing:

lab/w05_backpressure.py
hooks={"post_tool": lambda n, a, r:
       "VERIFICATION FAILED — fix before finishing" if "exit=1" in r else None}
real output — the trace
[ 0.000] tool  test({'fail': True})
[ 0.000] hook  {'name': 'test', 'injected': 'VERIFICATION FAILED — fix before finishing'}
[ 0.000]   ->  exit=1
              tests/test_auth.py::test_expired_token FAILED
              E   AssertionError: expected 401, got 200
              E   at auth/middleware.py:88
              [hook] VERIFICATION FAILED — fix before finishing
[ 0.000] tool  test({})
[ 0.000]   ->  exit=0
[ 0.000] say   Fixed auth/middleware.py:88, suite green.

The model cannot decline to see the injected line, cannot reason past it, and it appears whether or not the model was inclined to check. That combination — deterministic, unavoidable, legible — is what distinguishes a control from a suggestion.

Map it back to Module 00's grid: this is a computational sensor feeding an inferential actor. Cheap, reliable, and it runs on every single tool call.

Section 3An agent cannot grade its own work

The second half of the module, and the more surprising one.

When asked to evaluate work they've produced, agents tend to respond by confidently praising the work — even when, to a human observer, the quality is obviously mediocre.Anthropic

Part B of the walkthrough runs the same generator through two graders. Three landing-page drafts, escalating in quality; the grader decides when to stop.

real output · lab/w05_backpressure.py
self-evaluation
  passed after 1 round(s)
    round 1: score  9  Looks great! Clean, modern, professional. Ship it.
  shipped: Landing page: white bg, purple gradient hero, three feature cards…

separate + skeptical
  passed after 3 round(s)
    round 1: score  4  generic AI-slop pattern: 'purple gradient';
                       generic AI-slop pattern: 'three feature cards'…
    round 2: score  8  no stock-pattern tells; distinct point of view
    round 3: score 10  no stock-pattern tells; distinct point of view
  shipped: Landing page: near-black canvas, editorial type scale, full-bleed…

Self-evaluation shipped draft one with a 9/10 and a compliment. The skeptical evaluator forced two revisions.

Why separation works

It is not that the second grader is smarter. It is the same model. Three things changed:

  1. Separation. The grader has no investment in the artifact. It did not write it and gains nothing from its approval.
  2. Criteria. "Is this good?" became "does it violate these four named rules?" A subjective judgment became a checkable one.
  3. Calibration. Anthropic used few-shot examples with detailed score breakdowns, which aligned the evaluator's judgment with the author's and reduced score drift across iterations.
Anthropic's honest caveat

Separation alone does not eliminate leniency. The evaluator is still an LLM and still inclined to be generous toward LLM-generated output. Their actual claim is narrower and more useful: "tuning a standalone evaluator to be skeptical turns out to be far more tractable than making a generator critical of its own work." It is a better place to apply pressure, not a solved problem.

The four criteria

From their frontend design work, given to both generator and evaluator:

CriterionQuestion
Design qualityDoes it feel like a coherent whole rather than a collection of parts?
OriginalityEvidence of custom decisions, or template layouts and library defaults?
CraftTypography hierarchy, spacing, colour harmony, contrast. A competence check.
FunctionalityCan users find primary actions and complete tasks without guessing?

They weighted design quality and originality heavily, because Claude already scored well on craft and functionality by default. The criteria explicitly penalised generic patterns — the walkthrough's evaluator does the same, docking points for "purple gradient" and "three feature cards".

An unnerving detail: the wording steered outputs in ways they did not anticipate. Including "the best designs are museum quality" pushed generations toward a particular visual convergence. Your criteria are not a neutral measuring instrument; they are also a prompt.

Section 4What it costs

The full three-agent harness versus a single agent on the same prompt:

HarnessDurationCost
Solo20 min$9
Full harness (planner + generator + evaluator)6 hr$200

20x the cost. The difference in output was stark — the solo run produced an app whose central feature simply did not work, with the wiring between entity definitions and the game runtime broken and no surface indication of where. The harness run produced something playable.

But the honest conclusion is conditional, and it is the sentence to remember:

The evaluator is not a fixed yes-or-no decision. It is worth the cost when the task sits beyond what the current model does reliably solo.Anthropic

On Sonnet 4.5 that boundary was close, and the evaluator caught meaningful issues across the whole build. On Opus 4.6 the boundary moved outward: tasks that used to need evaluator oversight were handled well solo, and for those the evaluator became overhead. It kept earning its cost only at the edge of the generator's capability.

Tuning an evaluator is real work

Anthropic is blunt that this did not work out of the box: "Out of the box, Claude is a poor QA agent." Early runs identified legitimate issues, then talked themselves into deciding they were not a big deal and approved anyway. It also tested superficially rather than probing edge cases.

Their loop: read the evaluator's logs, find cases where its judgment diverged from yours, update the prompt to close that gap, repeat. Several rounds before it graded acceptably. Note that this is the ratchet from Module 00, applied to a harness component rather than to the code.

Sprint contracts

One more mechanism worth stealing. Before each chunk of work, generator and evaluator negotiated a contract: what "done" looks like, and how it will be verified — agreed before any code was written. The generator proposed, the evaluator reviewed, they iterated until agreement. Sprint 3 alone had 27 criteria for the level editor. This bridges the gap between a deliberately high-level spec and a testable implementation, without over-specifying the technical path upfront.

Exercises
  1. Add a fifth "tell" to the skeptical evaluator. Does draft #3 still pass? This is how criteria drift into over-fitting the grader.
  2. Make the evaluator hostile enough that nothing passes in three rounds. What does your harness do — loop forever, ship the best attempt, or escalate to a human? Implement your answer.
  3. Take your own test runner and make it silent on success. Measure the before and after in characters.
  4. Write four grading criteria for your actual domain, weighted. Then find a case where a well-scoring output is obviously bad, and fix the criteria. Repeat until you cannot.
